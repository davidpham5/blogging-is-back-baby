The Core Principle: Visual Language as a Contract

The Verge succeeds because each visual treatment makes an implicit promise to the reader. Image card with overlay text = "this is a news article, click to read." Dense text row = "this is a quick link, skim and move on." The reader never has to decide how to read something — the visual form tells them before they process the words.

The 5 Design Principles The Verge Applies

1. Gestalt Similarity

Items that look alike are grouped. Within each zone, every item uses the same visual template. The reader's eye finds a rhythm.

2. Information Scent

Each visual treatment promises a specific interaction. Image card = article. Text row = quick link. Breaking this forces the reader to stop and re-orient.

3. Column as Contract

Left/main = primary editorial weight. Right/sidebar = secondary, discovery. Width communicates importance before any content is read.

4. Cognitive Load

Consistent visual treatments within a zone mean the reader only processes what is said, not how to read it. Inconsistency adds invisible overhead.

5. Temporal vs. Curatorial Weight

StoryStream is temporal — it flows down as a thread, ordered by time. Turbolinks are curatorial — visually weighted, each one is a statement. These have incompatible reading rhythms. When interleaved, neither can establish momentum.

⚠️ One Critical Question Before We Split

You proposed: Turbolinks in left column, StoryStream in right column (under essays).

The Verge would raise a flag here: if StoryStream goes under essays in the same column, you're mixing your editorial voice (essays) with your curation voice (storystream) in the same zone. A reader might not know where essays end and storystream begins — are storystream entries short essays? Is this the same kind of writing?

The question to answer: Should StoryStream be in the right column at the same level as turbolinks (a true two-column homepage), or does it belong under essays because they share a writing/voice identity?
