The ones worth being able to name: not logging IP addresses against search queries — minimization applied at the architecture level rather than the policy level; random near-location for local search results, anonymous cookies for settings (geo coarsening + de-identified state); tracker blocking that runs locally on-device, so most blocking happens without sending browsing data back to DuckDuckGo

"the best way to protect personal information is to stop it being collected at all — is the whole point of minimization in one sentence"

reminds me of a reducer redux function: "_Just-in-time / progressive collection_ — ask for a field at the moment it's needed with a purpose string, instead of collecting everything up front "in case."

**k-anonymity — "is any single row specific enough to pin to a person?"**  
This one's about _singling out_. Take the columns an attacker could use to link you to outside knowledge — ZIP, age, sex, the "quasi-identifiers" — and generalize or suppress them until every row looks identical to at least k−1 others on those columns. Instead of "ZIP 20910, age 34, female" (maybe unique in your data), you release "ZIP 209**, age 30–39, female," and now 200 people share that description. You've hidden each person in a crowd of at least k.

the risk was never the data alone, it's the data _joined_ with something the attacker already has. The classic result is that ZIP + birthdate + sex uniquely identifies most Americans, so "anonymized" medical records get re-identified by joining on public voter rolls. k-anonymity is the direct answer — make those join keys non-unique.
